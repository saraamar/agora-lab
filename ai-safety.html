AI Safety Research · AGORA Lab · Bowling Green State University

What goes wrong when AI agents run the operation?

Supply chains are the oldest multi-agent systems we have, and the best-documented record of how sensible local decisions add up to harmful system-wide outcomes. AGORA Lab uses that record to study the safety of AI agents deployed in real operations, and how people keep meaningful oversight of them.

Why an operations engineer works on AI safety

Operations research has studied emergent failure for sixty years.

AI agents are moving from chat windows into procurement, inventory, scheduling and logistics, where decisions have physical and economic consequences and many agents interact at once. That is the setting operations research was built for.

AGORA Lab comes to AI safety from industrial and systems engineering rather than machine learning. What we bring is a toolkit for stress-testing interacting decision-makers, and access to the real deployment settings where those failures will show up first.

Emergent failure is the norm, not the exception. The bullwhip effect, cascading stockouts and supplier collapse all come from agents that each behave reasonably on their own. These are well-characterised, measurable failure modes we can test AI agents against.
Mature methods for system-level evaluation. Discrete-event simulation, game-theoretic inventory models, disruption stress tests and multi-criteria decision analysis transfer directly to evaluating agent populations.
Realistic deployment contexts. Small manufacturers and cooperatives will adopt AI agents with thin oversight and scarce data. That is where safety assumptions are weakest and least studied.
Field access. Active partners in Northwest Ohio manufacturing and across the MENA region make it possible to test findings outside the lab.

First experiment

Do LLM agents amplify or dampen supply-chain instability?

A small, fully reproducible starting point, built on the open-source MANGO Arena testbed. It tests a well-known failure mode with clear metrics before moving on to harder questions about collusion and oversight. The card below describes the full study design; the first pilot covers part of it.

Study design

Setup
A serial supply chain inside MANGO. Each stage is run by an LLM agent that sees only local inventory, orders and limited messages.
Shock
A demand surge or supplier failure injected mid-run.
Baselines
Classical ordering policies (base-stock) and student teams from MANGO pilots.
Variations
Model capability, information sharing, and a per-agent "safety threshold" instruction.
Metrics
Order-variance amplification (bullwhip ratio), service level, total cost, and violations of stated constraints.

Open questions

The questions we most want answered.

Do LLM agents amplify or dampen the bullwhip effect compared with humans and classical policies, and does this change as models become more capable?
A clean, measurable test of whether capability brings system-level stability or new instability.
In a shared market, do competing agents drift into tacit coordination, such as withholding supply or matching prices, without being told to?
Collusion between agents is hard to see from any single agent's behaviour.
When agents have different safety thresholds, does the least cautious agent set the risk level of the whole system?
This decides whether safety must be assured per agent or per system.
When data is scarce, which oversight designs (approval gates, uncertainty display, deferral rules) keep operators calibrated instead of rubber-stamping?
Oversight that looks meaningful on paper can fail quietly in practice.
Which operational decisions should never be fully delegated, and what evidence would tell us?
Connects the technical findings to deployment guidance for firms and policymakers.
